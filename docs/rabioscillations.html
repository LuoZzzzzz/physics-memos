Rabi oscillations

The time dependent Hamiltonian is:

$$ H(t) = H_0 + V(t) = \begin{bmatrix} E_1 & 0 \\ 0 & E_2 \end{bmatrix} + e\mathcal{E}_0 \cos(\omega t) \begin{bmatrix} \braket{1|z|1} & \braket{1|z|2} \\ \braket{2|z|1} & \braket{2|z|2} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} E_1 & \hbar\Omega \cos(\omega t) \\ \hbar\Omega \cos(\omega t) & E_2 \end{bmatrix} $$

where $\braket{1|z|1} = \braket{2|z|2} = 0$ by parity and the Rabi frequency is: $$\Omega = \frac{eE_0 |\braket{1|z|2}|}{\hbar}$$

Now, plug the solution $a_i(t) = c_i(t) e^{-\tfrac{iE_it}{\hbar}}$ into the time dependent Schrodinger equation $i\hbar \partial_t \ket{\psi(t)} = H(t)\ket{\psi(t)}$ and simplify. This will give: $$ \begin{align} \begin{bmatrix} \dot{c}_1(t) \\ \dot{c}_2(t) \end{bmatrix} &= -i \Omega \cos(\omega t) \begin{bmatrix} c_2(t) e^{-i(E_2-E_1)t/\hbar} \\ c_1(t) e^{i(E_2-E_1)t/\hbar} \end{bmatrix} \\ &= -i \frac{\Omega}{2} \begin{bmatrix} c_2(t) e^{i(\omega-\omega_0)t} + c_2(t) e^{-i(\omega+\omega_0)t} \\ c_1(t) e^{-i(\omega-\omega_0)t} + c_1(t) e^{i(\omega+\omega_0)t} \end{bmatrix} \\ &= -i \frac{\Omega}{2}\begin{bmatrix} c_2(t) e^{i \delta t} \\ c_1(t) e^{-i \delta t} \end{bmatrix} \end{align} $$ where we have discarded the $e^{\pm i (\omega_0 + \omega) t / \hbar}$ terms by rotating wave approximation. For this approximation to hold, the area under a $T_\text{fast}/2$ $\sin$ half period must be small relative to the slow varying change in the wavefunction.

Now, let $c_1(t) = \bar{c}_1(t) e^{i \delta t / 2}$, $c_2(t) = \bar{c}_2(t) e^{-i \delta t / 2}$. Again plugging these into the above gives: $$ \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \begin{bmatrix} \bar{c}_1(t) \\ \bar{c}_2(t) \end{bmatrix} = -\frac{i}{2} \begin{bmatrix} \delta & \Omega \\ \Omega & -\delta \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \bar{c}_1(t) \\ \bar{c}_2(t) \end{bmatrix} $$ We can solve this equation directly, but there's a slick way to see what is happening. Note that the right hand side may be decomposed into generators of rotations about the $z$ and $x$ axes: $$ i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \begin{bmatrix} \bar{c}_1(t) \\ \bar{c}_2(t) \end{bmatrix} = \frac{\hbar}{2} \left( \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \Omega \\ \Omega & 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} \delta & 0 \\ 0 & -\delta \end{bmatrix} \right) \begin{bmatrix} \bar{c}_1(t) \\ \bar{c}_2(t) \end{bmatrix} = \frac{\hbar}{2}(\Omega\sigma_x + \delta\sigma_z) \begin{bmatrix} \bar{c}_1(t) \\ \bar{c}_2(t) \end{bmatrix} $$ Now, rewrite:

$$\frac{\hbar}{2}(\Omega\sigma_x + \delta\sigma_z) = \begin{bmatrix} \Omega \\ 0 \\ \delta \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_x \\ \sigma_y \\ \sigma_z \end{bmatrix} = \frac{\hbar \sqrt{\Omega^2+\delta^2}}{2} \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{\Omega^2+\delta^2}} \begin{bmatrix} \Omega \\ 0 \\ \delta \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_x \\ \sigma_y \\ \sigma_z \end{bmatrix} \right) = \frac{\hbar\Omega_{\text{eff}}}{2} \underbrace{\hat{\Omega}_{\text{eff}}\cdot \vec{\sigma}}_{\text{generates rotations about } \hat{\Omega}_\text{eff}$} $$

The solution to this is obtained by exponentiating the generator: $$U(t) = e^{-i\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\Omega_\text{eff} t}_{\text{rotation angle}}\cdot(\hat{\Omega}_\text{eff} \cdot \vec{\sigma})}$$ So the vector $\begin{bmatrix} \bar{c}_1(t) \\ \bar{c}_2(t) \end{bmatrix}$ rotates about the axis $\hat{\Omega}_\text{eff}$ (on the Bloch sphere) at angular velocity $\Omega_\text{eff}$ (because the standard spin $1/2$ rotation by angle $\theta$ is $e^{-i(\theta /2) \hat{n}\cdot\vec{\sigma}})$.

Effect of driving phase when there is no detuning
Suppose $\delta=0$ and the system starts in the ground state $\ket{1}$. The coefficients $\bar{c}_1(t)$ and $\bar{c}_2(t)$ evolves as: $$ \begin{bmatrix} \bar{c}_1(t) \\ \bar{c}_2(t) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\left(\frac{\Omega t}{2}\right) \\ -i\sin\left(\frac{\Omega t}{2}\right) \end{bmatrix} $$ which when represented on the Bloch sphere corresponds to a rotation about the $x$ axis.

In this case we have considered the effect of a $\cos(\omega t)$ driving field acting on a system that is in state $\ket{1}$ at $t=0$. However, the driving field need not be synchronised with the system at $t=0$. That is, instead of driving using $\cos(\omega t)$, we could have driven the system with $\cos(\omega t + \Phi)$ where $Phi$ is some arbitrary initial phase representing the phase lag at $t=0$ between the phase of the electric field and the phase of the system (on the Bloch sphere). Note that this phase difference will change as the system evolves.

In this case the time evolution equation becomes $$ \begin{align} \begin{bmatrix} \dot{c}_1(t) \\ \dot{c}_2(t) \end{bmatrix} &= -i \Omega \cos(\omega t + \Phi) \begin{bmatrix} c_2(t) e^{-i(E_2-E_1)t/\hbar} \\ c_1(t) e^{i(E_2-E_1)t/\hbar} \end{bmatrix} \\ &\approx -\frac{i\Omega}{2}\begin{bmatrix} c_2(t) e^{i \delta t + i\Phi} \\ c_1(t) e^{-i \delta t - i\Phi} \end{bmatrix} \end{align}$$ Performing the substitution $c_1(t) = \bar{c}_1(t) e^{i\delta t/2 + i\Phi}$, $c_2(t) = \bar{c}_2(t) e^{-i\delta t/2 - i\Phi}$ gives: $$ \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \begin{bmatrix} \bar{c}_1(t) \\ \bar{c}_2(t) \end{bmatrix} = -\frac{i}{2} \begin{bmatrix} \delta & \Omega e^{-i\Phi} \\ \Omega e^{i \Phi} & -\delta \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \bar{c}_1(t) \\ \bar{c}_2(t) \end{bmatrix} $$ which gives the effective Schrodinger equation: $$ i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \begin{bmatrix} \bar{c}_1(t) \\ \bar{c}_2(t) \end{bmatrix} = \frac{\hbar}{2} \begin{bmatrix} \delta & \Omega e^{-i\Phi} \\ \Omega e^{i \Phi} & -\delta \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \bar{c}_1(t) \\ \bar{c}_2(t) \end{bmatrix} = \frac{\hbar}{2}(\Omega\cos(\Phi)\sigma_x + \Omega\sin(\Phi)\sigma_y) \begin{bmatrix} \bar{c}_1(t) \\ \bar{c}_2(t) \end{bmatrix} $$ corresponding to a rotation of angular velocity $\Omega$ about the axis $\begin{bmatrix} \cos(\Phi) \\ \sin(\Phi) \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ on the Bloch sphere.

Therefore, a rotation about the $y$ axis of the Bloch sphere may be achieved by driving an initial $\ket{1}$ state using a $\sin(\omega t)$ field instead of a $\cos(\omega t)$ field. If we add in a detuning $\delta$ then the effective Hamiltonian becomes: $$H_\text{eff} = \frac{\hbar}{2}(\Omega\cos(\Phi)\sigma_x + \Omega\sin(\Phi)\sigma_y + \delta \sigma_z)$$ Corresponding to a rotation about the axis $\begin{bmatrix} \Omega\cos(\Phi) \\ \Omega\sin(\Phi) \\ \delta \end{bmatrix}$ at angular frequency $\sqrt{\Omega^2 + \delta^2}$. If $\delta$ is very large, then the rotation actually becomes very rapid, but the amplitude will be very small. We can see this by noting that the rotation axis points very close to the initial direction of the system ($\ket{1}$) and because of that the state cannot stray very far from its initial position.

What does it mean to substitute $a_i(t) \leftarrow c_i(t)e^{-iE_i t/\hbar}$? What about $c_i(t) \leftarrow \bar{c}_i(t) e^{\pm i \delta t /2}$?
Consider the substitution $a_i(t) \leftarrow c_i(t)e^{-iE_i t/\hbar}$. Under this substitution, the state $\begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \end{bmatrix} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ corresponds to the state $\begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{bmatrix} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} e^{-i E_1 t / \hbar} \\ e^{-i E_2 t / \hbar} \end{bmatrix} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ e^{-i (E_2-E_1) t / \hbar} \end{bmatrix}$. So a state that is stationary in the $c_i(t)$ coordinates rotates $\ket{+x} \rightarrow \ket{-y} \rightarrow \ket{-x} \rightarrow \ket{+y}$ in the original $a_i(t)$ picture. We can therefore conclude that the change of coordinates corresponds to a change to a frame that rotates about the $z$ axis of the Bloch sphere at $\omega_0 = (E_2-E_1)/\hbar$.

The same logic tells us that a state $\begin{bmatrix} \bar{c}_1 \\ \bar{c}_2 \end{bmatrix} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ looks like $\begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \end{bmatrix} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ e^{-i \delta t} \end{bmatrix}$ in the original $c_i(t)$ coordinates, meaning that the $\bar{c}_i$ coordinates gives an additional angular velocity boost $\delta$ relative to the $c_i$ coordinates. So the $\bar{c}_i$ frame stacks on top of $c_i$, bringing the total rotation rate to $\omega_0 + \delta = \omega$. Physically, the $\bar{c}_i$ frame co-rotates with the drive, which is why the effective Hamiltonian​ in those coordinates is time-independent.

Broadband limit, from oscillations to linear rate
Consider a broadband spectrum (meaning $\delta \gg \Omega$) driving an atom. For a pure driving field the transition probability of the atom as a function of time is: $$\text{pr}_{1\rightarrow 2}(t) = \frac{\Omega^2}{\Omega^2+\delta^2} \sin^2\left(\frac{\sqrt{\Omega^2+\delta^2}}{2} t \right)$$ but for large detuning (applicable for a broadband spectrum) it is approximately: $$\text{pr}_{1\rightarrow 2}(t) = \frac{\Omega^2}{\delta^2} \sin^2\left(\frac{\delta}{2} t \right)$$ Since the broadband spectrum is incoherent (meaning the phase of any two components $\cos(\omega t)$ and $\cos(\omega' t)$ are random, and that the phase of a given frequency $\omega$ also jumps around randomly with time), the transition probabilities for all the different frequencies sum. So the total transition probability is: $$\text{pr}_{1\rightarrow 2}(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \Omega^2(\omega) \frac{\sin^2\left(\frac{\delta(\omega)}{2} t \right)}{\delta(\omega)^2} g(\omega) \,\text{d}\omega$$ where $g(\omega)$ is defined so that $\epsilon_0^2 g(\omega)$ tells us how $\epsilon_0^2$ is distributed over angular frequencies $\omega$. In the limit of a pure driving frequency we must have $\int \epsilon_0^2 g(\omega) \,\text{d}\omega = \epsilon_0^2$, so $g(\omega)$ must be a normalised distribution and have units of time. For large $t$, the $\text{sinc}^2$ function becomes a delta function: $$\frac{\sin^2\left(\frac{\delta(\omega)}{2} t \right)}{\delta(\omega)^2} \rightarrow \frac{\pi t}{2} \delta(\delta)$$ This holds provided $\Omega^2(w) g(w)$ varies slowly over the $\text{sinc}^2$ peak width ($\approx 1/t$). Hence: $$\text{pr}_{1\rightarrow 2}(t) \approx \frac{\pi t}{2} \Omega^2(\delta=0 \implies \omega_0) g(\omega_0)$$ Now, note that we can relate $M = \hbar \Omega$, where $M$ is the matrix element in Fermi's Golden rule ($M = \langle 1 | e \mathcal{E}_0 z | 2\rangle$). Actually, $M$ is conventionally defined to contain a single $e^{-i \omega t}$ oscillation, but here we have two of them ($e^{i \omega t}$ and $e^{-i\omega t}$ from the cosine), so we really need $2M = \hbar\Omega$. Further, note that $\rho(E) = g(\omega)/\hbar$, since the standard density in FGR is per unit energy, not frequency. Plugging these into the above equation gives: $$\text{pr}_{1\rightarrow 2}(t) \approx \frac{\pi t}{2} \left(\frac{2|M|}{\hbar}\right)^2 \hbar \rho(E_0) = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar} |M|^2 \rho(E_0) t = \Gamma t$$ and we have therefore recovered Fermi's Golden Rule in the context of a two state system driven by incoherent radiation. Note that in this context, $\rho$ is the normalised power spectrum (in units of inverse energy), not the density of states. However, the two becomes the same if the energy in each mode is the same, which is the case in classical blackbody radiation (a result of the equipartition theorem). Again, as a lecturer once said, you should not pull Fermi's Golden Rule out of your ass - you always derive it in the context in which you are applying it first.
