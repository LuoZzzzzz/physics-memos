    Fine structure of hydrogen

    Common hydrogen symbols
    $$ R_\infty = \frac{me^4}{64\pi^3\epsilon_0^2 \hbar^3c}, \phantom{x} \alpha = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0\hbar c} = 1/137, $$ $$ a_0 = \frac{4\pi \epsilon_0\hbar^2}{me^2} = 5.29 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m}, \phantom{x} \mu_B = \frac{e \hbar}{2m} = 9.24 \times 10^{-24} \text{ JT}^{-1} $$ $$R_\infty hc = \frac{1}{2}\alpha^2 mc^2 = 13.6 \text{ eV}$$

    Spin-orbit Hamiltonian
    $$H_{\text{so}} = -\vec{\mu}_e\cdot\vec{B}$$ The electron spin magnetic moment is $\vec{\mu}_e = -g_s \mu_B \vec{S}$, where $\vec{S}$ is dimensionless. The magnetic field produced by the nucleus at the electron's location may be obtained by: $$ \begin{align} \vec{B} &= - \frac{\vec{v} \times \vec{E}}{c^2} \\ &= - \frac{1}{c^2}\left(\vec{v}\times(\frac{Ze}{4\pi \epsilon_0}\frac{1}{r^3} \vec{r})\right) \\ &= - \frac{\hbar}{mc^2}\frac{Ze}{4\pi \epsilon_0}\frac{1}{r^3}\left(\frac{ m\vec{v}\times\vec{r}}{\hbar}\right) \\ &= \frac{\hbar}{mc^2}\frac{Ze}{4\pi \epsilon_0}\frac{1}{r^3} \vec{L} \end{align} $$ where $\vec{L}$ is dimensionless. So putting things together we find that: $$H_{\text{so}} = g_s \mu_B \frac{\hbar}{mc^2}\frac{Ze}{4\pi \epsilon_0}\frac{1}{r^3} \vec{S}\cdot\vec{L}$$ Now take expectation for a state $\ket{n, j, l, s}$. Using $\langle \frac{1}{r^3} \rangle = \frac{2}{l(l+1)(2l+1)}(\frac{Z}{n a_0})^3$, and accounting for Thomas precession $g_s \leftarrow (g_s-1) \approx 1$: $$\langle H_{\text{so}}\rangle = \mu_B \frac{\hbar}{mc^2}\frac{Ze}{4\pi \epsilon_0}\left(\frac{Z}{n a_0}\right)^3 \cdot \frac{j(j+1)-l(l+1)-s(s+1)}{l(l+1)(2l+1)}$$ Now, we would like to rewrite the spin-orbit shift in terms of $R_\infty hc$ and $\alpha$. This should be possible for all perturbations, because both $R_\infty hc$ and $\langle H \rangle$ have units of energy, while $\alpha$ is the only meaningful dimensionless parameter we can construct in this situation. We do this because $R_\infty hc$ is the characteristic energy scale of the hydrogen atom, while $\alpha$ is the characteristic of classical electromagnetism to special relativity, so putting things in this form lets us directly compare the scale of various interactions. Focus on the prefactor: $$ \begin{align} \mu_B \frac{\hbar}{mc^2} \frac{Ze}{4 \pi \epsilon_0} \left(\frac{Z}{n a_0}\right)^3 &= \frac{Z^3}{n^3}\frac{e\hbar}{2m}\frac{\hbar}{mc^2} \frac{Ze}{4 \pi \epsilon_0}\left(\frac{me^2}{4\pi \epsilon_0\hbar^2}\right)^3 \\ &= \frac{Z^4}{n^3}\frac{me^4}{32\pi^2\epsilon^2\hbar^2}\left(\frac{e^2}{4\pi \epsilon_0 \hbar c}\right)^2 \\ &= \frac{Z^4}{n^3}\frac{me^4}{32\pi^2\epsilon^2\hbar^2}\underbrace{\frac{hc}{2\pi\hbar c}}_{1}\left(\frac{e^2}{4\pi \epsilon_0 \hbar c}\right)^2 \\ &= \frac{Z^4}{n^3}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{me^4}{64\pi^3\epsilon^2\hbar^3c}}_{R_\infty}hc\cdot\underbrace{\left(\frac{e^2}{4\pi \epsilon_0 \hbar c}\right)^2}_{\alpha^2} \\ &= R_\infty hc \cdot \frac{Z^4\alpha^2}{n^3} \end{align}$$ And so: $$\langle H_{\text{so}}\rangle = R_\infty hc \cdot \frac{Z^4\alpha^2}{n^3} \cdot \frac{j(j+1)-l(l+1)-s(s+1)}{l(l+1)(2l+1)}$$

    Relativistic and Darwin corrections
    In addition to spin-orbit coupling, there are two other order $\alpha^2 R_\infty hc$ fine structure perturbations. $$\langle H_{\text{rel}} \rangle = - R_\infty hc \cdot \frac{\alpha^2}{n} \left( \frac{1}{l + \frac{1}{2}} - \frac{3}{4n} \right)$$ $$\langle H_{\text{Darwin}} \rangle = R_\infty hc \cdot \frac{Z^4 \alpha^2}{n^3} \cdot \frac{\delta_{l0}}{n} $$ Putting all corrections together gives $$\Delta E_{\text{fs}} = R_\infty hc \cdot \frac{Z^4 \alpha^2}{n^3}\left( \frac{1}{j + \tfrac{1}{2}} - \frac{3}{4n} \right)$$ which depends only on $n$ and $j$. This means, for example, that after fine structure perturbation $3s$ and $3p_{1/2}$ have the same energy, as do $3p_{3/2}$ and $3d_{3/2}$.

    Terms and levels

    A short calculation to show how terms and levels arise in divalent atoms. This scenario arises in the $2017$ exam, question $2$.

    6s7s
    $$ \begin{aligned} (\underbrace{0\otimes0}_{l_1 \otimes l_2}) \otimes (\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2} \otimes \tfrac{1}{2}}_{s_1 \otimes s_2}) &= (\underbrace{0}_L) \otimes (\underbrace{0 \oplus 1}_S) \\ &= (\underbrace{0 \otimes 0}_{{}^1S}) \oplus (\underbrace{0 \otimes 1}_{{}^3S}) \\ &= (\underbrace{0}_{{}^1S_0}) \oplus (\underbrace{1}_{{}^3S_1}) \\ \end{aligned} $$

    6s6p
    $$ \begin{aligned} (\underbrace{0\otimes1}_{l_1 \otimes l_2}) \otimes (\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2} \otimes \tfrac{1}{2}}_{s_1 \otimes s_2}) &= (\underbrace{1}_L) \otimes (\underbrace{0 \oplus 1}_S) \\ &= (\underbrace{1 \otimes 0}_{{}^1P}) \oplus (\underbrace{1 \otimes 1}_{{}^3P}) \\ &= (\underbrace{1}_{{}^1P_1}) \oplus (\underbrace{0 \oplus 1 \oplus 2}_{{}^3P_0, {}^3P_1,{}^3P_2}) \\ \end{aligned} $$ Consider the transitions between $S=1$ states. In the presence of a magnetic field, the upper ${}^3S_1$ term splits into $3$ $m_J$ levels. The lower ${}^3P_0, {}^3P_1, {}^3P_2$ levels split into $1, 3, 5$ levels respectively. The transitions ${}^3S_1 \rightarrow {}^3P_0, {}^3S_1 \rightarrow {}^3P_1, {}^3S_1 \rightarrow {}^3P_2$ then give rise to $3, 6, 9$ transition lines respectively. Remember these numbers - they show up quite often in these kind of problems. Note also that the transition ${}^1S_0 \rightarrow {}^3P_1$ has $6$, not $7$ lines because the $0 \rightarrow 0$ transition does not happen.

    Normal Zeeman
    The shift in a transition line is given by: $$\delta E_{\text{Zeeman}} = \mu_BB(g_u m_u - g_l m_l)$$ If the upper and lower levels have $S=0$ then they will have the same $g$ factor of $1$ ($g_u = g_l = 1$), then: $$\delta E_{\text{Zeeman}} = \mu_BB (1\cdot m_u - 1\cdot m_l) = \mu_BB \Delta m_J = \pm \mu_BB, 0$$ This is called the normal Zeeman effect. Whenever we have normal Zeeman effect, there are precisely three transition line shifts at $\pm \mu_BB, 0$.

    Note that the transition ${}^3S_1 \rightarrow {}^3P_0$, despite having $3$ shifts, is not a normal Zeeman effect, because the shifts are $\pm 2\mu_BB, 0$. Likewise, both ${}^3P_1$ and ${}^3P_2$ have $g_J = \frac{3}{2}$ but transitions between them are not normal. Note also, the statement "whenever we have a set of $3$ transition lines which are not spaced apart by $\mu_B B$ we can conclude that one of the levels is $J=0$" is false. A counterexample is ${}^3P_1$, ${}^3P_2$, $g_u = g_l = \frac{3}{2}$ just mentioned.

    Laser gain equation

    For laser light of frequency $\omega$ exciting a transition middle frequency $\omega_{21}$ $$ \begin{align} \text{power gained} &= \underbrace{[(N_2A\delta z)B_{21} - (N_1A\delta z)B_{12}]}_{\text{number of atoms in each level}} \cdot \underbrace{\hbar\omega}_{\text{photon energy}} \cdot \underbrace{G(\omega-\omega_{21})}_{\text{linewidth}} \cdot \rho(\omega) \\ &= B_{21}(N_2 - \frac{B_{12}}{B_{21}}N_1)A\delta z \cdot \hbar\omega \cdot G(\omega-\omega_{21}) \cdot \frac{1}{c}\,c\rho(\omega) \\ &= B_{21}\underbrace{(N_2 - \frac{g_2}{g_1}N_1)}_{g_1B_{12} = g_2B_{21}}A\delta z \cdot \hbar\omega \cdot G(\omega-\omega_{21}) \cdot \frac{1}{c}\,\underbrace{I(\omega)}_{I = c\rho} \\ &= (N_2 - \frac{g_2}{g_1}N_1)A\delta z \cdot \underbrace{B_{21} \frac{\hbar\omega}{c} \frac{\omega^2}{\pi^2c^2}}_{A_{21}}\frac{\pi^2c^2}{\omega^2} \cdot G(\omega-\omega_{21}) \cdot I(\omega) \\ &= \underbrace{(N_2 - \frac{g_2}{g_1}N_1)}_{N^*}A\delta z \cdot \underbrace{A_{21}\frac{\pi^2c^2}{\omega^2} \cdot G(\omega-\omega_{21})}_{\sigma(\omega-\omega_{21})} \cdot I(\omega) \\ &= N^* \cdot \sigma(\omega-\omega_{21}) \cdot I(\omega) \, A\delta z \end{align} $$

    Now, we need to find the population inversion density $N^*$ in terms of the system's parameters. Start with the rate equations, with pump rates $S_1$ and $S_2$ $$ \begin{align} \dot{N}_2 &= 0 = S_2 - \frac{N^* \sigma(\omega-\omega_{21}) I}{h \omega} - \frac{N_2}{\tau_2} = S_2 - N^* W - \frac{N_2}{\tau_2} \\ \dot{N}_1 &= 0 = S_1 + N^* W - \frac{N_1}{\tau_1} + N_2 A_{21} \end{align} $$ where we have substituted $W = \frac{\sigma(\omega-\omega_{21}) I}{h \omega}$.

    Now, isolate $N_1$ and $N_2$ $$ \begin{align} N_2 &= \tau_2 (S_2 - N^* W) \\ N_1 &= \tau_1 \left(S_1 + N^* W + N_2 A_{21} \right) \\ &= \tau_1 \left(S_1 + N^* W + A_{21} \tau_2 (S_2 - N^* W) \right) \\ &= \tau_1 \left(S_1 + (1 - A_{21} \tau_2) N^* W + A_{21} \tau_2 S_2 \right) \end{align} $$

    Then, use $N_1$ and $N_2$ to form an equation for the population inversion density $N^*$ $$ \begin{align} N^* &= N_2 - \frac{g_2}{g_1}N_1 \\ &= \tau_2 (S_2 - N^* W) - \frac{g_2}{g_1} \tau_1 \left( S_1 + (1 - A_{21} \tau_2) N^* W + A_{21} \tau_2 S_2 \right) \end{align} $$ Finally isolate and $N^*$ $$ \begin{align} N^* &= \frac{ \tau_2 S_2 \left( 1 - \frac{g_2}{g_1} \tau_1 A_{21} \right) - \frac{g_2}{g_1} \tau_1 S_1}{1 + \left[ \tau_2 + \frac{g_2}{g_1} \tau_1 (1 - A_{21} \tau_2) \right] \frac{\sigma(\omega-\omega_{21}) I}{h \omega}} \end{align} $$
