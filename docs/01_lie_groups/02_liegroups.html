    Generators of a Lie group

    Consider a continuous family of (linear) transformations $D(\alpha)$, smoothly labelled by real parameters $\alpha = (\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\dots)$, with $D(0)=1$ (the identity transformation). In a neighbourhood of the identity we can Taylor-expand: $$D(\delta\alpha) = 1 + i\,\delta\alpha_a X_a + \mathcal{O}(\delta\alpha^2), \qquad X_a \equiv -i\left.\frac{\partial D(\alpha)}{\partial \alpha_a}\right|_{\alpha=0}.$$ (Repeated indices are summed.) The operators $X_a$ are the generators of the group: they describe what an infinitesimal transformation looks like. The factor of $i$ is a convention, chosen so that generators of unitary transformations come out Hermitian (see below). We take the $X_a$ to be linearly independent; otherwise some of the parameters would be redundant. Let $N$ be their number.

    The exponential parametrisation

    Why exponentiate?

    Knowing $D$ only for tiny $\delta\alpha$ isn't very useful on its own. The standard trick for turning “infinitesimal” data into a finite transformation is to apply the infinitesimal step many times in a row, making each step smaller as you add more of them — exactly how $e^x$ is built from compound interest.

    We therefore define a new labelling of group elements, the exponential parametrisation: $$D(\alpha) \equiv \lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{i\alpha_aX_a}{n}\right)^n = e^{i\alpha_aX_a}.$$ This is a choice of how the parameters $\alpha$ label group elements, not a consequence of the original labelling; from now on $D(\alpha)$ means $e^{i\alpha_aX_a}$.

    Is $e^{i\alpha_aX_a}$ really a group element?

    $1+i\alpha_aX_a$ is, in general, not an element of the group for finite $\alpha$ — it is only the first-order approximation to one. But $1+\dfrac{i\alpha_aX_a}{n}$ differs from a genuine group element, the element with parameters $\alpha/n$ in the original labelling, only at order $1/n^2$. Multiplying $n$ such factors together accumulates an error of order $n\cdot\tfrac{1}{n^2}=\tfrac1n\to0$. So $e^{i\alpha_aX_a}$ is the limit of products of genuine group elements, and is itself an element of the group for every $\alpha$ — not just to first order.

    We call $\{X_a\}$ the generators of the Lie group $D(\alpha)$.

    Where structure constants come from

    Generators of a non-commutative (“non-abelian”) Lie group don't commute with each other, so in general $$e^{i\alpha_aX_a}e^{i\beta_bX_b} \neq e^{i(\alpha_a+\beta_a)X_a}.$$ Nonetheless, the left-hand side is a product of two group elements, hence itself a group element. For small $\alpha$ and $\beta$ it is close to the identity, so it can be written as $e^{i\delta_aX_a}$ for some $\delta$ depending on $\alpha$ and $\beta$. The question is: what is $\delta$?

    Strategy

    Take $\ln$ of both sides and expand in powers of the generators. This is the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff (BCH) approach: it turns a hard question about multiplying exponentials into an easier one about expanding a logarithm.

    Write $u \equiv \alpha_aX_a$ and $v\equiv \beta_bX_b$ for brevity, so we want $\delta$ with $i\delta_aX_a=\ln(e^{iu}e^{iv})=\ln(1+K)$, where $K\equiv e^{iu}e^{iv}-1$. Expanding each exponential to second order and multiplying: $$K = \Big(1+iu-\tfrac12u^2\Big)\Big(1+iv-\tfrac12v^2\Big) - 1 + \mathcal{O}(3) = iu+iv-uv-\tfrac12u^2-\tfrac12v^2+\mathcal{O}(3).$$ Now use $\ln(1+K)=K-\tfrac12K^2+\mathcal{O}(K^3)$. Since $K$ starts at first order, only its first-order part is needed in $K^2$: $$K^2 = (iu+iv)^2 + \mathcal O(3) = -u^2-uv-vu-v^2 + \mathcal O(3).$$ Putting these together: $$\begin{aligned} \ln(1+K) &= \Big[iu+iv-uv-\tfrac12u^2-\tfrac12v^2\Big] - \tfrac12\Big[-u^2-uv-vu-v^2\Big] + \mathcal O(3)\\ &= iu+iv - uv + \tfrac12uv+\tfrac12vu + \mathcal O(3) = iu+iv-\tfrac12[u,v] + \mathcal O(3). \end{aligned}$$

    What just happened

    All the $u^2$ and $v^2$ terms cancelled — the only thing left over from “squaring” the exponentials is the commutator $[u,v]$. This is the second-order truncation of the full BCH formula, and it's the reason commutators are the central object in Lie theory: to second order, the “leftover” from composing two group elements is entirely a commutator.

    So $i\delta_aX_a = iu+iv-\tfrac12[u,v]+\mathcal O(3)$. Rearranging, $$[u,v] = [\alpha_aX_a,\beta_bX_b] = -2i(\delta_c-\alpha_c-\beta_c)X_c + \mathcal O(3).$$ $\delta_c-\alpha_c-\beta_c$ starts at second order in $\alpha,\beta$; write its second-order part (the terms of degree two in $\alpha$ and $\beta$ jointly) as $-\tfrac12\gamma_c$. The left-hand side is exactly of second order, so matching second-order terms on both sides gives $$[\alpha_aX_a,\beta_bX_b] = i\gamma_cX_c,$$ exactly, as an identity between polynomials in $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

    Extracting the structure constants. The commutator is bilinear — linear in each argument separately — so the parameters come straight out of the bracket, exactly: $$\alpha_a\beta_b\,[X_a,X_b] = i\gamma_cX_c\qquad\text{for all }\alpha,\beta.$$ Now choose $\alpha$ pointing along a single direction $a$ and $\beta$ along a single direction $b$, i.e. $\alpha=s\,\hat e_a$ and $\beta=t\,\hat e_b$. The left-hand side becomes $st\,[X_a,X_b]$, while the right-hand side is some linear combination of the $X_c$. So $[X_a,X_b]$ is itself a linear combination of the generators: the generators are closed under commutation. This is a consequence of the group being closed under multiplication, not an extra assumption. The coefficients depend only on $a,b,c$, since there is no $\alpha$ or $\beta$ anywhere in $[X_a,X_b]$. Call them $if_{abc}$:

    Structure constants

    $$[X_a,X_b] = if_{abc}X_c.$$

    Why must $\gamma$ take this specific structure?

    Substituting back gives $\gamma_c=\alpha_a\beta_bf_{abc}$. This bilinear form is not an extra assumption. $\gamma_c$ is a second-order polynomial in $\alpha$ and $\beta$, which could a priori contain $\alpha\alpha$ and $\beta\beta$ terms as well. But the left-hand side $\alpha_a\beta_b[X_a,X_b]$ is exactly bilinear, and the $X_c$ are linearly independent, so each $\gamma_c$ must be bilinear too, with coefficients $f_{abc}$ that cannot depend on $\alpha$ or $\beta$. Both closure and the constants $f_{abc}$ come out of the argument.

    Since $[X_b,X_a]=-[X_a,X_b]$ (antisymmetry of the commutator itself), comparing coefficients of the linearly independent $X_c$ gives $$f_{bac} = -f_{abc} \qquad \text{(the structure constants are antisymmetric in their first two indices).}$$ And comparing coefficients of $X_c$ in $i\delta_aX_a=iu+iv-\tfrac12[u,v]$ gives $\delta$ explicitly: $$\delta_c = \alpha_c+\beta_c-\tfrac12\alpha_a\beta_bf_{abc}+\cdots$$

    Why the structure constants are enough

    A natural worry: if we expanded $\ln(e^{iu}e^{iv})$ to third order instead of second, might we find terms like $X_1X_1X_2$ or $X_1^3$ that cannot be built from any combination of nested commutators? To match the left-hand side $i\delta_aX_a$, which is linear in the generators, we would then need additional constraints such as $X_a^3=c_bX_b$ — a whole new set of “third-order structure constants” on top of the $f_{abc}$'s.

    No — the BCH theorem rules this out

    The Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff theorem guarantees that $\ln(e^{iu}e^{iv})$, expanded to any order, is always a sum of nested commutators of $u$ and $v$ — never a stand-alone term like $X_1^3$. Each nested commutator reduces, by repeated use of $[X_a,X_b]=if_{abc}X_c$, to a linear combination of the $X_c$ whose coefficients are built from $\alpha$, $\beta$ and the $f_{abc}$. So the commutation relations determine $\delta_a$ uniquely, as an infinite series in $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $f_{abc}$ alone: no further “structure constants” need to be specified. Going to higher order adds new terms to the series, but at every order the result is an exact linear combination of the $X_a$.

    Unitary representations force real structure constants

    Symmetries in quantum mechanics are represented by unitary operators (so that probabilities are preserved), so it matters what unitarity implies for the generators. If every $e^{i\alpha_aX_a}$ is unitary, then differentiating $\big(e^{i\alpha_aX_a}\big)^\dagger e^{i\alpha_aX_a}=1$ at $\alpha=0$ gives $-iX_a^\dagger+iX_a=0$: each $X_a$ is Hermitian. Taking the dagger of the structure-constant relation, $$[X_a,X_b]^\dagger = (if_{abc}X_c)^\dagger = -if_{abc}^*X_c.$$ But also, using $(X_aX_b)^\dagger=X_b^\dagger X_a^\dagger=X_bX_a$, $$[X_a,X_b]^\dagger = X_bX_a-X_aX_b = -[X_a,X_b] = -if_{abc}X_c.$$ Comparing coefficients of the linearly independent $X_c$:

    $$f_{abc}^* = f_{abc} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{the structure constants of a unitary representation are real.}$$

    The Jacobi identity and the adjoint representation

    Any commutator of an associative product satisfies the Jacobi identity; it is an algebraic identity, not a separate assumption:

    Jacobi identity

    $$[X_a,[X_b,X_c]] + [X_b,[X_c,X_a]] + [X_c,[X_a,X_b]] = 0.$$

    Expanding the first term using the structure constants twice, $$[X_a,[X_b,X_c]] = [X_a,if_{bcd}X_d] = if_{bcd}[X_a,X_d] = if_{bcd}\cdot if_{ade}X_e = -f_{bcd}f_{ade}X_e,$$ and cyclically permuting $(a,b,c)$ for the other two terms, the Jacobi identity becomes (by linear independence of the $X_e$) a purely numerical identity among the structure constants, with free indices $a,b,c,e$:

    $$f_{bcd}f_{ade} + f_{cad}f_{bde} + f_{abd}f_{cde} = 0.$$

    The adjoint representation. Define $N\times N$ matrices $T_a$ by reading the structure constants off as matrix entries: $$[T_a]_{bc}\equiv-if_{abc}.$$ Then $$[T_a,T_b]_{pq} = [T_a]_{pd}[T_b]_{dq} - [T_b]_{pd}[T_a]_{dq} = -f_{apd}f_{bdq} + f_{bpd}f_{adq}.$$ The Jacobi identity above with $c\to p$ and $e\to q$ reads $f_{bpd}f_{adq} + f_{pad}f_{bdq} + f_{abd}f_{pdq} = 0$. Using antisymmetry in the first two indices, $f_{pad}=-f_{apd}$ and $f_{pdq}=-f_{dpq}$, so $$-f_{apd}f_{bdq} + f_{bpd}f_{adq} = f_{abd}f_{dpq} = if_{abd}\,\big(-if_{dpq}\big) = if_{abd}\,[T_d]_{pq}.$$ Hence:

    The adjoint representation

    $$[T_a,T_b] = if_{abc}T_c.$$ The matrices $T_a$ obey the same commutation relations as the generators, so they generate a representation of the group built from the structure constants alone, acting on an $N$-dimensional space (one dimension per generator). Up to a transpose and a sign, which disappear once $f_{abc}$ is totally antisymmetric (below), $T_a$ is the matrix of the map $X\mapsto[X_a,X]$: each generator is represented by its own action, through the bracket, on all the generators.

    A scalar product on the generators

    From here on, $T_a$ denotes the representation of $X_a$ in the adjoint representation. On the linear space of these matrices, define the scalar product $$\langle T_a,T_b\rangle \equiv \operatorname{Tr}[T_aT_b] \equiv M_{ab}.$$ This is the Killing form $\operatorname{Tr}[\operatorname{ad}X_a\,\operatorname{ad}X_b]$, evaluated on the Hermitian generators $X_a$. (Mathematicians usually evaluate it on the anti-Hermitian combinations $iX_a$, which flips its sign; that is why “compact” corresponds to positive values here and to negative ones in maths texts.) The indices $a,b$ run from $1$ to $N$, so the scalar products form an $N\times N$ matrix $M$, which is

    • symmetric, because $\operatorname{Tr}[T_aT_b]=\operatorname{Tr}[T_bT_a]$ (cyclicity of the trace);
    • real, because the $f_{abc}$'s are real (for a unitary representation), which makes each $T_a$ purely imaginary, and a product of two purely imaginary matrices has real entries.

    Changing the basis of generators

    The generators are only defined up to a change of basis. Consider a real, invertible $N\times N$ matrix $L$ and new generators $$X_a\to X_a'=L_{ab}X_b.$$ ($L$ is real so that Hermitian generators stay Hermitian and the structure constants stay real.) Then $$[X_a',X_b'] = L_{ad}L_{be}[X_d,X_e] = iL_{ad}L_{be}f_{deg}X_g = iL_{ad}L_{be}f_{deg}\big(L^{-1}\big)_{gc}X_c',$$ using $X_g=\big(L^{-1}\big)_{gc}X_c'$. So the structure constants transform as $$f_{abc}\to f'_{abc} = L_{ad}L_{be}f_{deg}\big(L^{-1}\big)_{gc}.$$ The new structure constants generate a new adjoint representation: $$[T_a']_{bc} = -if'_{abc} = L_{ad}\,L_{be}\,[T_d]_{eg}\,\big(L^{-1}\big)_{gc} = L_{ad}\,\big[LT_dL^{-1}\big]_{bc}, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad T_a' = L_{ad}\,\big(LT_dL^{-1}\big).$$ So a linear transformation of the generators induces two operations on the adjoint representation: the same linear recombination $T_d\to L_{ad}T_d$, together with a similarity transformation $T\to LTL^{-1}$ of each matrix.

    Do the two operations commute?

    Yes. Conjugation is linear, and the $L_{ad}$ are just numbers: $$L\Big(\sum_d L_{ad}T_d\Big)L^{-1} = \sum_d L_{ad}\,\big(LT_dL^{-1}\big).$$ Recombining and then conjugating gives the same result as conjugating and then recombining.

    The effect on the scalar product follows from cyclicity of the trace: $$\operatorname{Tr}[T_a'T_b'] = L_{ac}L_{bd}\operatorname{Tr}\big[LT_cL^{-1}\,LT_dL^{-1}\big] = L_{ac}L_{bd}\operatorname{Tr}\big[LT_cT_dL^{-1}\big] = L_{ac}L_{bd}\operatorname{Tr}[T_cT_d],$$ i.e. $$M\to M' = LML^T.$$ (The matrices $T_c$, $T_d$ cannot be rearranged inside the trace, but the numbers $L_{ac}$, $L_{bd}$ can be moved anywhere.)

    Compact Lie algebras

    Diagonalising the scalar product. $M$ is real and symmetric, so it can be diagonalised by an orthogonal matrix: $M=Q\Lambda Q^T$. Choosing $L=Q^T$, $$\begin{gathered} M\to M' = Q^T\big(Q\Lambda Q^T\big)Q = \Lambda,\qquad \Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(k^1,k^2,\dots,k^N),\\ \text{i.e.}\qquad \operatorname{Tr}[T_a'T_b'] = k^a\,\delta_{ab}\quad(\text{no sum}). \end{gathered}$$ A further diagonal $L=\operatorname{diag}(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_N)$ rescales $k^a\to \mu_a^2\,k^a$: by varying $L$ we can change the size of each $k^a$, but not its sign, since $L$ appears twice in $M\to LML^T$. More generally, by Sylvester's law of inertia, no real change of basis changes the numbers of positive, negative and zero $k^a$, so the definition below does not depend on the basis.

    Compact Lie algebra

    If every $k^a$ is positive, the Lie algebra is called compact. In that case we can rescale every $k^a$ to a single common constant $\lambda>0$, by an appropriate choice of $L$, giving $$\operatorname{Tr}[T_aT_b]=\lambda\,\delta_{ab}.$$ From here on we work in such a basis (and drop the primes).

    In this basis, the structure constants can be recovered directly from the trace: $$\operatorname{Tr}\big[[T_a,T_b]T_c\big] = if_{abd}\operatorname{Tr}[T_dT_c] = if_{abd}\,\lambda\,\delta_{dc} = i\lambda f_{abc} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad f_{abc} = -\frac{i}{\lambda}\operatorname{Tr}\big[[T_a,T_b]T_c\big].$$

    The structure constants are completely antisymmetric

    By the cyclic property of the trace, $$\operatorname{Tr}\big[[T_a,T_b]T_c\big] = \operatorname{Tr}[T_aT_bT_c - T_bT_aT_c] = \operatorname{Tr}[T_bT_cT_a - T_cT_bT_a] = \operatorname{Tr}\big[[T_b,T_c]T_a\big],$$ so $f_{abc}=f_{bca}$. Combined with the antisymmetry $f_{abc}=-f_{bac}$ already established, $$f_{abc}=f_{bca}=f_{cab} \quad\text{(cyclic)}, \qquad f_{abc}=-f_{bac}=-f_{cba}=-f_{acb} \quad\text{(swap any two)}:$$ swapping any two indices flips the sign. This holds in the basis where $\operatorname{Tr}[T_aT_b]=\lambda\delta_{ab}$; a generic change of basis destroys it. Orthogonal changes of basis ($LL^T=1$) preserve $\operatorname{Tr}[T_aT_b]=\lambda\delta_{ab}$, and hence total antisymmetry. In this basis each $T_a$ is purely imaginary and antisymmetric, hence Hermitian, and $T_a$ is exactly the matrix of $X\mapsto[X_a,X]$.

    Invariant subalgebras and simple Lie algebras

    Invariant subalgebra

    A subset of generators spans an invariant subalgebra if bracketing any of its elements with any generator of the whole algebra always lands back inside it: if $X$ is in the invariant subalgebra and $Y$ is any generator, then $[X,Y]$ is again in the invariant subalgebra. By linearity, the same then holds for any combination $Y$ of generators.

    Exponentiating an invariant subalgebra produces an invariant subgroup. Let $X$ be in the invariant subalgebra and $Y$ be any element of the algebra. Conjugation by an invertible matrix commutes with taking powers, $S^{-1}X^nS=(S^{-1}XS)^n$, and hence with exponentiation, so $$\big(e^{iY}\big)^{-1}e^{iX}e^{iY} = e^{iX'},\qquad X'\equiv e^{-iY}Xe^{iY}.$$ Expanding to second order, $$\begin{aligned} e^{-iY}Xe^{iY} &= \Big(1-iY-\tfrac12Y^2\Big)\,X\,\Big(1+iY-\tfrac12Y^2\Big) + \mathcal O(3)\\ &= X - iYX + iXY - \tfrac12Y^2X + YXY - \tfrac12XY^2 + \mathcal O(3)\\ &= X - i(YX-XY) - \tfrac12\big(Y^2X-2YXY+XY^2\big) + \mathcal O(3)\\ &= X - i[Y,X] - \tfrac12[Y,[Y,X]] + \mathcal O(3). \end{aligned}$$ At every order the correction is a nested commutator $[Y,[Y,\dots[Y,X]]]$, and each such commutator lands back in the invariant subalgebra by definition. So $X'$ is in the invariant subalgebra too, $e^{iX'}$ is in the corresponding subgroup, and conjugation by any group element $e^{iY}$ cannot push you out of it: the subgroup is invariant.

    Every Lie algebra has two trivial invariant subalgebras: $\{0\}$ (zero), and the whole algebra.

    Simple algebra

    A Lie algebra is simple if it has no invariant subalgebras other than these two trivial ones. (A single generator, as for $U(1)$, trivially qualifies; it is conventionally excluded, and “simple” is reserved for non-abelian algebras.)

    Simple vs. irreducible — don't confuse the two

    Simple is a statement about the generators and their commutators (an algebraic property). Irreducible is a statement about a representation of the group elements and the vector space it acts on — whether that space contains a smaller invariant subspace. The theorem below relates the two notions for one particular representation: the adjoint representation.

    Simple + compact ⇒ the adjoint representation is irreducible

    Theorem

    If a Lie algebra is (1) simple, and (2) compact, so that in a suitable basis $\operatorname{Tr}[T_aT_b]=\lambda\delta_{ab}$, then its adjoint representation is irreducible.

    Setting. The adjoint representation acts on $N$-component vectors $\xi$, one component per generator. Work in the compact basis, where $f_{abc}$ is totally antisymmetric. There $[T_a]_{cb}=-if_{acb}=if_{abc}$, so $T_a\xi$ lists the coefficients of $[X_a,\xi_bX_b]=if_{abc}\xi_bX_c$: $T_a$ is the matrix of $X\mapsto[X_a,X]$. Reducibility means there is a subspace $W$, with $0\lt\dim W\lt N$, that every $T_a$ maps into itself. We take $W$ to be spanned by real vectors $\xi$, i.e. by real combinations of generators; complex subspaces are discussed after the proof.

    Proof. We argue by contradiction. Suppose the adjoint representation were reducible: there is an invariant subspace $W$ of dimension $m$, with $0\lt m\lt N$. Choose a real orthonormal basis of the generator space whose first $m$ vectors span $W$. This is an orthogonal change of basis, so $\operatorname{Tr}[T_aT_b]=\lambda\delta_{ab}$ and total antisymmetry survive. In the new basis $W$ is spanned by $X_1,\dots,X_m$. Let $r$ label the first group, $r\in\{1,\dots,m\}$, and $x$ the second, $x\in\{m+1,\dots,N\}$. Invariance of $W$ says $T_a$ never maps an $r$-direction into an $x$-direction: $$[T_a]_{xr} = -if_{axr} = 0\qquad\text{for every } a,\ x,\ r.$$ The index $a$ must itself be one of the $x$'s or one of the $r$'s, so both $f_{x_1x_2r}=0$ and $f_{r_1xr_2}=0$. By total antisymmetry, every structure constant carrying indices from both groups is a permutation of one of these, so it vanishes too. The only non-zero $f_{abc}$'s are of the form $f_{r_1r_2r_3}$ or $f_{x_1x_2x_3}$, and every $T_a$ is block-diagonal: $$[T_a] = \left[\begin{array}{c|c} m\times m \text{ block} & 0 \\ \hline 0 & (N-m)\times(N-m) \text{ block} \end{array}\right].$$

    So the whole algebra breaks into two non-trivial invariant subalgebras: bracketing any $X_r$ with any $X_x$ gives zero, and bracketing it with another $X_r$ stays inside the first group. $\{X_1,\dots,X_m\}$ (corresponding to $T_1$ to $T_m$) is therefore a non-trivial invariant subalgebra, and likewise $\{X_{m+1},\dots,X_N\}$ (corresponding to $T_{m+1}$ to $T_N$). This contradicts simplicity, which allows no non-trivial invariant subalgebras. So the adjoint representation cannot be reducible. ∎

    The idea in one sentence

    Reducibility of the adjoint representation would mean the generators split into two blocks that never interact under the bracket — but two non-interacting blocks are, by definition, each an invariant subalgebra, which simplicity forbids. Compactness enters through total antisymmetry. It converts the invariance of $W$, $f_{axr}=0$ (a statement about the matrices $T_a$), into $f_{rax}=0$ (a statement about the brackets $[X_r,X_a]$), and it makes the complementary block vanish as well.

    Real versus complex invariant subspaces

    The proof uses a real orthogonal change of basis, so it shows that no invariant subspace is spanned by real combinations of generators. The statement also holds for complex invariant subspaces, which is what matters in quantum mechanics, but that needs one more fact: for a compact simple algebra, allowing complex combinations of generators creates no new invariant subalgebras. Here compactness is essential. The Lorentz algebra is simple as a real algebra, yet over the complex numbers its adjoint representation splits in two: the self-dual and anti-self-dual parts of an antisymmetric tensor $F_{\mu\nu}$.

    $U(1)$ factors

    $U(1)$ factor

    A $U(1)$ factor of a group is an invariant subalgebra containing a single generator, which commutes with every other generator of that group.

    If $X_a$ is a $U(1)$ generator, then $[X_a,X_b]=if_{abc}X_c=0$ for every $b$, so $f_{abc}=0$ for every $b,c$. Since $[T_a]_{bc}=-if_{abc}$, this means $T_a$ is, according to the construction above, the zero matrix in the adjoint representation. Two consequences:

    1. The scalar product cannot see it. Row $a$ of $M_{ab}=\operatorname{Tr}[T_aT_b]$ vanishes, so $M$ has a zero eigenvalue, and by Sylvester's law some $k^a=0$ in every diagonal basis. So an algebra with a $U(1)$ factor is never compact in the strict sense defined above (every $k^a>0$); that definition really describes compact algebras without $U(1)$ factors (compact semisimple algebras). For example, $SU(2)\times U(1)$, with generators $\tfrac12\sigma_{1,2,3}$ and $\tfrac12\mathbb 1$, gives $\operatorname{Tr}[T_aT_b]=\operatorname{diag}(2,2,2,0)$.
    2. In every slot. Where $f_{abc}$ is totally antisymmetric, the vanishing of $f_{abc}$ with the $U(1)$ index in the first slot implies it vanishes in every slot: $$f_{abc}=f_{cab}=f_{bca}=f_{bac}=f_{cba}=f_{acb}=0,$$ so $X_a$ also never appears on the right-hand side of any commutator. Compact groups with $U(1)$ factors, such as $U(2)$, do admit a basis with totally antisymmetric $f_{abc}$ — for instance the basis above — but it comes from a positive-definite invariant scalar product other than $\operatorname{Tr}[T_aT_b]$, which vanishes on the $U(1)$ direction.
    What it means that structure constants “see” nothing about $U(1)$ factors

    A $U(1)$ generator contributes only zeros to the structure-constant array (in a totally antisymmetric basis) — exactly what an absent direction would give. So the list of non-zero $f_{abc}$'s can never reveal how many $U(1)$ factors a Lie algebra has, and the scalar product $\operatorname{Tr}[T_aT_b]$ built from them is equally blind to them. This is why, in classifying compact Lie algebras, the non-abelian (“semisimple”) part is fixed by its structure constants, while the number of $U(1)$ factors must be supplied as separate data.
