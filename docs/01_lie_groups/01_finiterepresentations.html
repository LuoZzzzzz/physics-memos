    Group definition

    Definition — Group

    A set $G$ with a binary operation is a group if:

    1. If $g, h \in G$ then $gh \in G$ — closure
    2. $(gh)k = g(hk)$ for all $g,h,k\in G$ — associativity
    3. There exists $e \in G$ such that $e g = g e = g$ for every $g\in G$ — identity
    4. For every $g \in G$ there exists $g^{-1} \in G$ such that $g g^{-1} = g^{-1} g = e$ — inverse

    Throughout, $G$ is a finite group with $n$ distinct elements $g_1, g_2, \dots, g_n$.

    The group table

    $$ \begin{array}{c|ccc} & g_1 & g_2 & \cdots \\ \hline g_1 & g_1^2 & g_1 g_2 & \cdots \\ g_2 & g_2 g_1 & g_2^2 & \cdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \end{array} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \text{entry in row } i,\ \text{column } j \text{ is } g_i g_j, $$ with the left factor taken from the row header and the right factor from the column header.

    Important property
    • Each row of the group table is a permutation of the $n$ group elements $\{g_1, g_2, \dots, g_n\}$.
    • Each column of the group table is also a permutation of $\{g_1, g_2, \dots, g_n\}$.

    Equivalently, every group element appears in each row and in each column exactly once. (Proved in the next section.)

    Consequence: location matrices. For each element $g$, put a $1$ at every position of the table where the entry is $g$, and $0$ everywhere else: $$P(g)_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } g_ig_j = g,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$ Since $g$ appears exactly once in each row and each column, every $P(g)$ is a permutation matrix. The group table therefore decomposes into $n$ permutation matrices, one for each element, recording its $n$ locations in the table.

    Open question 1

    How are these permutation matrices related to the row/column permutation matrices? (Answered in the final section.)

    Every element appears once in each row and column

    Claim

    $g_i g_1, g_i g_2, \dots, g_i g_n$ (the $i^{\text{th}}$ row) contains exactly one copy of each group element.

    Fact 1: all $n$ elements $g_ig_1$ to $g_ig_n$ are distinct. Suppose otherwise, i.e. suppose that for some $a \neq b$, $$g_i g_a = g_i g_b.$$ Left-multiply both sides by $g_i^{-1}$ and use associativity and the identity, $g_i^{-1}(g_ig_a) = (g_i^{-1}g_i)g_a = eg_a = g_a$: $$g_i g_a = g_i g_b \quad\xrightarrow{\ \text{left-multiply by }g_i^{-1}\ }\quad g_i^{-1} g_i g_a = g_i^{-1} g_i g_b \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g_a = g_b.$$ But the column headers list each element once, so $a\neq b$ means $g_a\neq g_b$. This is a contradiction, so our premise is wrong: all $n$ elements of the row are distinct.

    Intuition: left multiplication by $g_i$, the map $g\mapsto g_ig$, can be undone by the map $g\mapsto g_i^{-1}g$. A map that can be undone cannot send two different elements to the same one.

    Fact 2: the row contains $n$ elements. Each entry lies in $G$ (closure), and there are $n$ of them. So: (1) no duplicates, all elements distinct; and (2) $n$ elements in total, drawn from a set of $n$ $\Rightarrow$ the row contains the entire group, each element exactly once.

    Columns. The same reasoning applies to the $i^{\text{th}}$ column, $g_1 g_i, g_2 g_i, \dots, g_n g_i$, using right multiplication instead: if $g_ag_i = g_bg_i$, right-multiplying by $g_i^{-1}$ gives $(g_ag_i)g_i^{-1} = g_a(g_ig_i^{-1}) = g_a$, and likewise $g_b$, so $g_a=g_b$.

    A simple $n$-dimensional representation

    Definition — Representation

    A representation of $G$ assigns to each element $g$ an invertible matrix $D(g)$ such that the matrices multiply like the group elements: $$D(g_i)\,D(g_j) = D(g_ig_j)\qquad\text{for all } i,j.$$ In particular $D(e)=I$, since $D(e)D(e)=D(e)$ and $D(e)$ is invertible.

    For any finite group $g_1, g_2, \dots, g_n$, there is a very easy $n$-dimensional representation we can construct.

    Procedure
    1. Map elements to vectors: $g_1 \to \mat{1\\0\\\vdots}$, $g_2 \to \mat{0\\1\\\vdots}$, etc. Write $V(g_j)$ for the $j^{\text{th}}$ standard basis vector.
    2. Associate to $g_i$ the matrix $M(g_i)$ defined by $$M(g_i)\, V(g_j) = V(g_i g_j)\qquad\text{for all } j.$$

    This fixes every column: column $j$ of $M(g_i)$ is $V(g_ig_j)$, so $M(g_i)$ is row $i$ of the group table written as unit vectors. By the important property, row $i$ contains each element once, so the columns of $M(g_i)$ are the $n$ basis vectors, each once: $M(g_i)$ is a permutation matrix. It is the matrix of the row-$i$ permutation $g_j\mapsto g_ig_j$. (This is called the regular representation.)

    Example. $$ \begin{array}{c|ccc} & g_1 & g_2 & g_3 \\ \hline g_1 & g_3 & g_1 & g_2 \\ g_2 & g_1 & g_2 & g_3 \\ g_3 & g_2 & g_3 & g_1 \end{array} \quad \Big\} \ \text{some arbitrary group table I made up} $$ This table does satisfy the group axioms. Row 2 and column 2 reproduce the headers, so $g_2=e$. Also $g_1g_3=g_3g_1=g_2$, so $g_3=g_1^{-1}$, and $g_1g_1=g_3$. It is the cyclic group of order 3, with $g_1=a$, $g_2=e$, $g_3=a^2$.

    So $V(g_1) = \mat{1\\0\\0}$, $V(g_2) = \mat{0\\1\\0}$, $V(g_3) = \mat{0\\0\\1}$.

    Row 1 then says: $$M(g_1) = \mat{0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0}.$$ For example, $M(g_1) V(g_1) = V(g_1 g_1) = V(g_3)$ fixes the first column of $M(g_1)$: $$\mat{0 & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times \\ 1 & \times & \times} \mat{1\\0\\0} = \mat{0\\0\\1},$$ and $V(g_1g_2)=V(g_1)$, $V(g_1g_3)=V(g_2)$ fix the second and third columns.

    Row 2 and Row 3 give: $$M(g_2) = \mat{1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1} \qquad M(g_3) = \mat{0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0}$$ As it must, the identity $g_2$ is represented by the identity matrix.

    Checking the construction

    In the construction, top headers $\to$ vectors, left headers $\to$ matrices. But the definition of a representation involves only the matrices, so let's check it directly: $$M(g_1) M(g_1) = \mat{0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0} \mat{0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0} = \mat{0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0} = M(g_3) = M(g_1g_1).$$ ✓ Seems to work.

    Open question 2

    Why does the construction (which is done in terms of both matrices and vectors) generalise to matrices only, i.e. extend to the full group structure?

    Resolution

    For any $k$, $$\begin{aligned} M(g_i)M(g_j)\,V(g_k) &= M(g_i)\,V(g_jg_k) = V\big(g_i(g_jg_k)\big)\\ &= V\big((g_ig_j)g_k\big) = M(g_ig_j)\,V(g_k), \end{aligned}$$ where the third equality uses associativity. The two matrices $M(g_i)M(g_j)$ and $M(g_ig_j)$ agree on every basis vector $V(g_k)$, so they are equal: $$M(g_i)M(g_j)=M(g_ig_j)\qquad\text{for all } i,j.$$ So $M$ is a representation of every finite group, not just of this example. The vectors only serve to define the matrices; once defined, the matrices inherit the multiplication law of the group through associativity.

    Location matrices revisited

    Random thought. From this construction we found: $$g_1 \to \mat{0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0}, \qquad g_2 \to \mat{1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1}, \qquad g_3 \to \mat{0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0}.$$ If we used instead the location matrices $P(g)$, based on the locations of $g_1, g_2, g_3$ in the group table, we get: $$g_1 \to \mat{0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1} \; ?, \qquad g_2 \to \mat{0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0} \; ?, \qquad g_3 \to \mat{1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0} \; ?$$

    Open question 3

    Is this a valid representation, and how are these matrices related to the representation we found?

    Check. $$P(g_1) P(g_1) = \mat{0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1} \mat{0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1} = \mat{1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1}, \quad \text{not in the set!}$$ In particular it is not $P(g_1g_1)=P(g_3)$, so this is not a valid representation. A quicker way to see it: any representation sends the identity to the identity matrix, but $P(g_2)=P(e)\neq I$. Comparing the two sets of matrices, the $P$'s look like mirror images of the $M$'s.

    Hypothesis

    They actually are mirror images?

    Resolution — the exact relation

    Compare the defining conditions entry by entry: $$P(g)_{ij}=1 \iff g_ig_j = g \iff g_i = g\,g_j^{-1},\qquad M(g)_{ij}=1 \iff g_i = g\,g_j.$$ So column $j$ of $P(g)$ is the column of $M(g)$ belonging to $g_j^{-1}$ rather than $g_j$. In matrix form, $$P(g) = M(g)\,P(e),$$ where $P(e)_{ij}=1 \iff g_ig_j=e$: $P(e)$ is the permutation matrix of the inversion map $g_j\mapsto g_j^{-1}$. Right-multiplying by $P(e)$ re-orders the columns of $M(g)$, sending column $j$ to the position of $g_j^{-1}$.

    • Mirror images? Only because of the labelling in this example. With $g_2=e$, inversion fixes $g_2$ and swaps $g_1\leftrightarrow g_3$, so $P(e)$ is the anti-diagonal matrix, which reverses the column order: a left–right mirror image. Had we labelled the identity $g_1$, inversion would swap only columns 2 and 3, and no mirror symmetry would be visible. The relation $P(g)=M(g)P(e)$ holds for every finite group.
    • Valid representation? $P$ is a representation if and only if $P(e)=I$, i.e. if and only if every element is its own inverse. If $P(e)=I$, then $P(g)=M(g)$ exactly, as for $\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_2$; if not, $P$ fails $D(e)=I$. Here $g_1^{-1}=g_3\neq g_1$, so it is not.
    • Open question 1. The row permutation matrices are the $M(g_i)$ themselves: row $i$ of the table is the permutation $g_j\mapsto g_ig_j$. The column permutation matrices $C(g_j)$, defined by $C(g_j)V(g_i)=V(g_ig_j)$, describe column $j$. The location matrices are built from either: $P(g) = M(g)\,P(e) = P(e)\,C(g)^{-1}$.
