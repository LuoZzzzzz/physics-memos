The algebra of the rotation group is about as small as a non-abelian Lie algebra can be: three generators and one commutation relation. Yet its representations are the reason spin comes in integers and half-integers, the reason a spin-jjj particle has 2j+12j+12j+1 states, and the reason angular momentum is quantized at all. This memo derives all of that from the commutation relations alone, using nothing more than an orthonormal basis and some patience.

The idea is a ladder. Find the state with the largest value of J3,J_3\text{,}J3​, and step down from it one rung at a time. The only subtle point is that the top rung might hold several states at once. So we’ll carry a degeneracy label α\alphaα along with us, and show at the end that irreducibility gets rid of it.

1The su(2)su(2)su(2) algebra

A Lie algebra with generators Xa,X_a\text{,}Xa​, for a=1,…,M,a=1,\dots,M\text{,}a=1,…,M, where MMM is the dimension of the algebra, satisfies

[Xa,Xb]=ifabcXc,[X_a,X_b] = if_{abc}X_c ,[Xa​,Xb​]=ifabc​Xc​,
(1)

with structure constants fabcf_{abc}fabc​ that are antisymmetric in aaa and b.b\text{.}b. For a compact group like SU(2)SU(2)SU(2) the generators can be chosen so that fabcf_{abc}fabc​ is totally antisymmetric (memo 2), and we’ll assume that from now on.

A totally antisymmetric fabcf_{abc}fabc​ vanishes whenever two of its indices are equal. For M=1M=1M=1 or M=2,M=2\text{,}M=2, every fabcf_{abc}fabc​ has a repeated index, so the algebra is abelian. The smallest MMM with a non-trivial fabcf_{abc}fabc​ is therefore M=3,M=3\text{,}M=3, and there the only totally antisymmetric three-index object is, up to normalization, the Levi-Civita symbol: fabc=λεabc.f_{abc}=\lambda \varepsilon_{abc}\text{.}fabc​=λεabc​. Rescaling the generators by 1/λ1/\lambda1/λ sets λ=1,\lambda=1\text{,}λ=1, and leaves

[Ja,Jb]=iεabcJc.[J_a,J_b] = i\varepsilon_{abc}J_c .[Ja​,Jb​]=iεabc​Jc​.
(2)

This commutation relation defines the Lie algebra su(2)su(2)su(2) of the group SU(2).SU(2)\text{.}SU(2).11 We avoid the letters iii and jjj as indices: iii is −1,\sqrt{-1}\text{,}−1​, and jjj will soon be the largest eigenvalue of J3.J_3\text{.}J3​.

2Setting up a representation

Suppose the algebra has a d×dd\times dd×d irreducible representation, acting on a ddd-dimensional vector space.22 Irreducibility is used only in Section 8. Everything before that holds for any finite-dimensional unitary representation. We take the representation to be unitary, so that J1,J_1\text{,}J1​, J2J_2J2​ and J3J_3J3​ are Hermitian.

We want an orthonormal basis of the space that’s adapted to the JJJ’s. Each JaJ_aJa​ is Hermitian, so each one on its own has an orthonormal eigenbasis. But no two of them commute (unless the representation is trivial, with every Ja=0),J_a=0\text{),}Ja​=0), so they can’t be diagonalized at the same time. So we can make the eigenstates of one, and only one, of them our basis. We pick J3.J_3\text{.}J3​.

Let jjj be the largest eigenvalue of J3,J_3\text{,}J3​, which exists because ddd is finite, and write the corresponding states as ∣j,α⟩,\ket{j,\alpha}\text{,}∣j,α⟩, where α\alphaα is a degeneracy index:

J3∣j,α⟩=j∣j,α⟩.J_3\ket{j,\alpha} = j\ket{j,\alpha}.J3​∣j,α⟩=j∣j,α⟩.
(3)
