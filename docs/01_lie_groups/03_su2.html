Irreducible Representations of $SU(2)$

23–25 September 2026
Digitised from handwritten notes.

Contents

    The $su(2)$ algebra

    Definition — Structure constants

    A Lie algebra with generators $X_a$ ($a=1,\dots,M$, where $M$ is the dimension of the algebra) satisfies $$[X_a,X_b] = i f_{abc}X_c ,$$ with structure constants $f_{abc}$ antisymmetric in $a,b$. For compact groups such as $SU(2)$ the generators can be chosen so that $f_{abc}$ is totally antisymmetric, and we assume this from now on.

    A totally antisymmetric $f_{abc}$ vanishes whenever two indices coincide. For $M=1$ or $M=2$ every $f_{abc}$ has a repeated index, so the algebra is abelian. The smallest $M$ with a non-trivial $f_{abc}$ is therefore $M=3$, and there the only totally antisymmetric three-index object is, up to normalisation, the Levi-Civita symbol: $f_{abc}=\lambda\,\varepsilon_{abc}$. Rescaling the generators by $1/\lambda$ sets $\lambda=1$.

    Definition — The $su(2)$ algebra

    $$[J_a,J_b] = i\varepsilon_{abc}J_c .$$ This commutation relation defines the Lie algebra $su(2)$ of $SU(2)$. (The letters $i$ and $j$ are avoided as indices: $i$ denotes $\sqrt{-1}$, and $j$ will denote the largest $J_3$ eigenvalue.)

    Setting up a representation

    Suppose the algebra has a $d\times d$ irreducible representation acting on a $d$-dimensional vector space. (Irreducibility is only used in the final section; everything before it holds for any finite-dimensional representation of the kind described here.) We take the representation to be unitary, so that $J_1,J_2,J_3$ are Hermitian.

    We want an orthonormal basis (ONB) of the space adapted to the $J$'s:

    1. each $J_a$ is Hermitian, so each on its own has an orthonormal eigenbasis;
    2. no two of them commute (unless the representation is trivial, $J_a=0$), so they are not simultaneously diagonalisable.

    We can therefore make the eigenstates of one and only one of them our ONB. We pick $J_3$.

    Let $j$ be the largest eigenvalue of $J_3$ (it exists since $d$ is finite), and denote the corresponding state(s) by $\ket{j,\alpha}$, where $\alpha$ is a degeneracy index: $$J_3\ket{j,\alpha} = j\ket{j,\alpha}.$$ If $j$ is degenerate, we choose an orthonormal basis of its eigenspace (Gram–Schmidt), so that $$\braket{j,\alpha}{j,\beta} = \delta_{\alpha\beta}.$$

    Ladder operators

    Definition — Ladder operators

    $$J^+ = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(J_1+iJ_2), \qquad J^- = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(J_1-iJ_2).$$ Since $J_1,J_2$ are Hermitian, $(J^+)^\dagger = J^-$.

    From $[J_a,J_b]=i\varepsilon_{abc}J_c$, i.e. $[J_3,J_1]=iJ_2$ and $[J_3,J_2]=-iJ_1$: $$\begin{aligned} {[J_3,J^\pm]} &= \frac{1}{\sqrt2}[J_3,J_1] \pm \frac{i}{\sqrt2}[J_3,J_2] = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(iJ_2)\pm\frac{i}{\sqrt2}(-iJ_1)\\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(iJ_2 \pm J_1\big) = \pm\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(J_1\pm iJ_2\big) = \pm J^\pm , \end{aligned}$$ and, using $[J_1,J_2]=iJ_3$, $$[J^+,J^-] = \tfrac12\big[J_1+iJ_2,\;J_1-iJ_2\big] = \tfrac12\big(-i[J_1,J_2] + i[J_2,J_1]\big) = \tfrac12\big(J_3+J_3\big) = J_3 .$$

    Result

    $$[J_3,J^\pm] = \pm J^\pm, \qquad\qquad [J^+,J^-] = J_3, \qquad\qquad (J^\pm)^\dagger = J^\mp .$$

    Hence, for a state $\ket{m}$ with $J_3\ket{m}=m\ket{m}$, $$J_3\big(J^\pm\ket{m}\big) = J^\pm J_3\ket m \pm J^\pm\ket m = (m\pm1)\,J^\pm\ket m .$$ So $J^\pm\ket m$ is either zero or an (unnormalised) eigenstate of $J_3$ with eigenvalue $m\pm1$: $J^\pm$ raise and lower the $J_3$ eigenvalue by one unit.

    The top of the ladder

    $J^+$ annihilates the top. $J^+\ket{j,\alpha}$ would have $J_3$ eigenvalue $j+1$, but $j$ is the largest eigenvalue. Hence $$J^+\ket{j,\alpha} = 0 \qquad\text{for all }\alpha.$$

    One step down. Apply $J^-$ to $\ket{j,\alpha}$ and write $$J^-\ket{j,\alpha} = N_j(\alpha)\,\ket{j-1,\alpha},$$ where $\ket{j-1,\alpha}$ is normalised and $N_j(\alpha)$ absorbs the norm of $J^-\ket{j,\alpha}$. Using $(J^-)^\dagger=J^+$, $J^+J^- = [J^+,J^-]+J^-J^+ = J_3+J^-J^+$, and $J^+\ket{j,\alpha}=0$: $$N_j^*(\beta)N_j(\alpha)\,\braket{j-1,\beta}{j-1,\alpha} = \bra{j,\beta}J^+J^-\ket{j,\alpha} = \bra{j,\beta}J_3\ket{j,\alpha} = j\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}.$$ This gives two things at once:

    1. for $\alpha\neq\beta$, $\ket{j-1,\alpha}$ and $\ket{j-1,\beta}$ are orthogonal;
    2. for $\alpha=\beta$, $\big\|J^-\ket{j,\alpha}\big\|^2=|N_j(\alpha)|^2=j$. In particular $j\ge0$, and we may choose the same real, positive constant for every $\alpha$: $$N_j(\alpha)=N_j=\sqrt j,\qquad \ket{j-1,\alpha}=\frac{J^-\ket{j,\alpha}}{N_j}.$$ (If $j=0$, then $J^-\ket{j,\alpha}=0$ and the ladder has a single rung; see termination.)

    Raising back up. $$J^+\ket{j-1,\alpha} = \frac1{N_j}J^+J^-\ket{j,\alpha} = \frac1{N_j}\big(J_3+J^-J^+\big)\ket{j,\alpha} = \frac{j}{N_j}\ket{j,\alpha} = N_j\ket{j,\alpha}.$$

    Result — first step of the ladder

    $$J^-\ket{j,\alpha} = N_j\ket{j-1,\alpha}, \qquad J^+\ket{j-1,\alpha} = N_j\ket{j,\alpha},\qquad N_j=\sqrt j,$$ and the states $\ket{j-1,\alpha}$ are orthonormal.

    Induction down the ladder

    We now repeat the argument at a general level, working through the algebra for $$J^-\ket{j-k,\alpha} = N_{j-k}(\alpha)\,\ket{j-k-1,\alpha}\qquad(k\ge1),$$ for as long as $J^-\ket{j-k,\alpha}\neq0$, i.e. $N_{j-k}\neq0$, so that the normalised state $\ket{j-k-1,\alpha}$ exists. (When it vanishes, the ladder ends; see termination.)

    The logic is as follows. The algebra needs three “requirements” to hold. At the start they are only established for the pair of levels $j/j{-}1$. The algebra then shows that if they hold for the pair $j{-}k{+}1/j{-}k$, they also hold for $j{-}k/j{-}k{-}1$. Hence $j/j{-}1$ holds $\Rightarrow$ $j{-}1/j{-}2$ holds $\Rightarrow\cdots$.

    The requirements at step $k$

    Req. 1 $J^+\ket{j-k,\alpha} = N_{j-k+1}\ket{j-k+1,\alpha}$, with $N_{j-k+1}$ real, positive and independent of $\alpha$.

    Req. 2 $\braket{j-k,\beta}{j-k,\alpha} = \delta_{\alpha\beta}$: the states we lower from are orthonormal.

    Req. 3 $\braket{j-k+1,\beta}{j-k+1,\alpha} = \delta_{\alpha\beta}$: the states one level above are orthonormal.

    The induction step. Using $J^+J^-=J_3+J^-J^+$ and, in the second term, $J^-J^+=(J^+)^\dagger J^+$: $$\begin{aligned} & N_{j-k}^*(\beta)N_{j-k}(\alpha)\,\braket{j-k-1,\beta}{j-k-1,\alpha} = \bra{j-k,\beta}J^+J^-\ket{j-k,\alpha}\\ &\qquad= \bra{j-k,\beta}J_3\ket{j-k,\alpha} + \big(J^+\ket{j-k,\beta}\big)^\dagger\big(J^+\ket{j-k,\alpha}\big)\\ &\qquad= (j-k)\,\braket{j-k,\beta}{j-k,\alpha} + N_{j-k+1}^2\,\braket{j-k+1,\beta}{j-k+1,\alpha}\\ &\qquad= \big[(j-k) + N_{j-k+1}^2\big]\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}. \end{aligned}$$ The third line uses Req. 1 on both the bra and the ket of the second term; the last line uses Req. 2 and Req. 3. This establishes, for the level $j-k-1$:

    • A. $\ket{j-k-1,\alpha}$ and $\ket{j-k-1,\beta}$ are orthogonal for $\alpha\neq\beta$.
    • B. Taking $\alpha=\beta$ and demanding $\ket{j-k-1,\alpha}$ be normalised, $|N_{j-k}(\alpha)|^2=(j-k)+N_{j-k+1}^2$ for every $\alpha$. Choosing $N_{j-k}$ real, positive and $\alpha$-independent, and dropping the argument $\alpha$, $$N_{j-k}^2 - N_{j-k+1}^2 = j-k .$$
    • C. $J^+\ket{j-k-1,\alpha} = N_{j-k}\ket{j-k,\alpha}$. Proof: by Req. 1 and then the defining relation $J^-\ket{j-k+1,\alpha}=N_{j-k+1}\ket{j-k,\alpha}$ from the previous step, we have $J^-J^+\ket{j-k,\alpha}=N_{j-k+1}^2\ket{j-k,\alpha}$. So $$\begin{aligned} J^+\ket{j-k-1,\alpha} &= \frac{1}{N_{j-k}}\big(J_3+J^-J^+\big)\ket{j-k,\alpha}\\ &= \frac{(j-k)+N_{j-k+1}^2}{N_{j-k}}\ket{j-k,\alpha} = N_{j-k}\ket{j-k,\alpha}, \end{aligned}$$ using B in the last step.

    Closing the induction. A, B, C are exactly the requirements for the next step, $k\to k+1$: $$\text{B and C}\;\Rightarrow\;\text{Req. 1},\qquad \text{A and B}\;\Rightarrow\;\text{Req. 2},\qquad \text{Req. 2 at step }k\;\Rightarrow\;\text{Req. 3 at step }k+1 .$$ For the base case $k=1$ (the pair $j/j{-}1$):

    • Req. 1: $J^+\ket{j-1,\alpha}=N_j\ket{j,\alpha}$ — shown at the top of the ladder;
    • Req. 2: $\braket{j-1,\beta}{j-1,\alpha}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}$ — shown at the top of the ladder;
    • Req. 3: $\braket{j,\beta}{j,\alpha}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}$ — by construction.

    The first round therefore gives $\braket{j-2,\beta}{j-2,\alpha}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}$, $N_{j-1}^2-N_j^2=j-1$ and $J^+\ket{j-2,\alpha}=N_{j-1}\ket{j-1,\alpha}$, and so on down the ladder for as long as it continues. The result at the top of the ladder, $N_j^2=j$, is the same recursion at $k=0$ with $N_{j+1}\equiv0$.

    What has been established so far

    For each $\alpha$ there is a ladder of orthonormal states, connected by the same real positive constants for every $\alpha$: $$\ket{j,\alpha}\ \overset{J^-,\,N_j}{\underset{J^+,\,N_j}{\rightleftharpoons}}\ \ket{j-1,\alpha}\ \overset{J^-,\,N_{j-1}}{\underset{J^+,\,N_{j-1}}{\rightleftharpoons}}\ \ket{j-2,\alpha}\ \cdots\quad \ket{j-k+1,\alpha}\ \overset{J^-,\,N_{j-k+1}}{\underset{J^+,\,N_{j-k+1}}{\rightleftharpoons}}\ \ket{j-k,\alpha}$$ with $N_{j-k}^2-N_{j-k+1}^2=j-k$ and $N_j=\sqrt j$.

    Closed form for the normalisation

    Telescoping the recursion, $$\begin{aligned} N_{j-k}^2 &= N_{j-k+1}^2 + (j-k) = N_{j-k+2}^2 + (j-k+1)+(j-k) = \cdots\\ &= \sum_{n=0}^{k}(j-n) = j+(j-1)+\cdots+(j-k) = \tfrac12(k+1)(2j-k), \end{aligned}$$ so, taking the positive root, $$N_{j-k} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\sqrt{(k+1)(2j-k)} .$$ Substituting $m=j-k$ (so $k=j-m$):

    Result — normalisation constant

    $$N_m = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\sqrt{(j+m)(j-m+1)}$$ is the constant for lowering from $\ket{m}$, or raising to $\ket{m}$: $$J^-\ket{m,\alpha}=N_m\ket{m-1,\alpha},\qquad J^+\ket{m-1,\alpha}=N_m\ket{m,\alpha}.$$

    Choosing every $N_m$ real and positive is the standard (Condon–Shortley) phase convention.

    Termination and the allowed values of $j$

    The representation has finite dimension $d$. Each lowering produces an eigenstate of $J_3$ with a new eigenvalue, hence orthogonal to all the previous ones, so the process must terminate. Suppose the $(\ell+1)$-th lowering is the first to give zero: $$J^-\ket{j-\ell,\alpha}=0 .$$ Since the $N_m$ do not depend on $\alpha$, neither does $\ell$. The induction step computes the norm of $J^-\ket{j-\ell,\alpha}$ without needing the state below to exist: $$\big\|J^-\ket{j-\ell,\alpha}\big\|^2 = \bra{j-\ell,\alpha}J^+J^-\ket{j-\ell,\alpha} = (j-\ell)+N_{j-\ell+1}^2 = \tfrac12(\ell+1)(2j-\ell).$$ This vanishes if and only if $2j-\ell=0$, since $\ell+1\neq0$. So $\ell=2j$: the bottom of the ladder is $m=j-\ell=-j$, consistent with $N_{-j}=0$ in the closed form.

    Result — allowed values of $j$

    $2j=\ell$ is a non-negative integer, so $$j\in\big\{0,\tfrac12,1,\tfrac32,2,\dots\big\},$$ and each ladder has exactly $2j+1$ states, $m=j,j-1,\dots,-j$.

    In fact positivity alone forces this. The squared norm $\tfrac12(\ell+1)(2j-\ell)$ is positive for $0\le\ell\lt 2j$, so the ladder cannot stop early. If $2j$ were not an integer, it would become negative at the first integer $\ell\gt 2j$, which is impossible for a squared norm.

    Open question

    Why does $J^-$ annihilate a state only at the end of a computation, when $J^+$ annihilated the top state $\ket{j,\alpha}$ directly?

    Resolution — the asymmetry is only in the order of construction

    $J^+\ket{j,\alpha}=0$ was an input: it is what “top of the ladder” means, and the closed form for $N_m$ was derived from it. The closed form is consistent with that input. Using $J^-J^+=J^+J^--J_3$, $$\big\|J^+\ket{m,\alpha}\big\|^2 = \bra{m,\alpha}\big(J^+J^--J_3\big)\ket{m,\alpha} = N_m^2-m = \tfrac12(j-m)(j+m+1) = N_{m+1}^2 ,$$ so $\big\|J^\pm\ket{m,\alpha}\big\|^2=\tfrac12(j\mp m)(j\pm m+1)$. The factor $(j-m)$ that makes $J^+$ vanish at $m=j$ ($N_{j+1}=0$) is the mirror image of the factor $(j+m)$ that makes $J^-$ vanish at $m=-j$ ($N_{-j}=0$). Had we started from the smallest eigenvalue and built the ladder upwards, $J^-\ket{-j,\alpha}=0$ would have been the input, and $J^+$ would have annihilated the top only at the end of the computation.

    Irreducibility and the standard spin-$j$ representation

    Each ladder is an invariant subspace. Fix $\alpha$ and consider the span of $\ket{m,\alpha}$, $m=j,\dots,-j$. $J_3$ is diagonal on it; $J^+$ and $J^-$ map it into itself (including $J^+\ket{j,\alpha}=0$ and $J^-\ket{-j,\alpha}=0$); and $$J_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(J^++J^-\big),\qquad J_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2\,i}\big(J^+-J^-\big),$$ so it is invariant under $J_1,J_2,J_3$, and hence under the group elements $e^{i\theta_1J_1},e^{i\theta_2J_2},e^{i\theta_3J_3}$. Different $\alpha$ give mutually orthogonal ladders: states at the same level with different $\alpha$ are orthogonal (conclusion A of the induction), and states at different levels have different $J_3$ eigenvalues.

    Irreducibility. By assumption the representation is irreducible, so its only non-zero invariant subspace is the whole space. Each ladder is therefore the whole space, so there is only one value of $\alpha$, and we drop the label: $d=2j+1$. Conversely, a single ladder is itself irreducible. Any invariant subspace of it is invariant under $J_3$, whose eigenvalues on the ladder are non-degenerate, so it is spanned by some of the $\ket{m,\alpha}$. Since $J^\pm$ connect neighbouring states with non-zero coefficients ($N_m\neq0$ for $-j\lt m\le j$), it must contain all of them.

    (For a reducible representation the ladders built from the top eigenvalue need not fill the space. The orthogonal complement of their span is also invariant, since the $J_a$ are Hermitian, and can be treated in the same way starting from its own largest $J_3$ eigenvalue. The space breaks up into orthogonal invariant subspaces, each a spin-$j$ irrep of the kind below.)

    Standard notation

    $$\ket{j,m}, \qquad \underbrace{j}_{\text{largest }J_3\text{ eigenvalue}}\ ,\qquad \underbrace{m}_{J_3\text{ eigenvalue of this state}}.$$

    The spin-$j$ representation is defined by its matrix elements: $$\begin{aligned} \big[J_3^{(j)}\big]_{m'm} &= \bra{j,m'}J_3\ket{j,m} = m\,\delta_{m'm},\\ \big[J^{+(j)}\big]_{m'm} &= \bra{j,m'}J^+\ket{j,m} = N_{m+1}\,\delta_{m',m+1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\sqrt{(j+m+1)(j-m)}\;\delta_{m',m+1},\\ \big[J^{-(j)}\big]_{m'm} &= \bra{j,m'}J^-\ket{j,m} = N_{m}\,\delta_{m',m-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\sqrt{(j+m)(j-m+1)}\;\delta_{m',m-1}, \end{aligned}$$ with $J_1^{(j)},J_2^{(j)}$ following from $J^{\pm(j)}$ as above. These matrices do represent the algebra: $[J_3,J^\pm]=\pm J^\pm$ because $J^\pm$ shift $m$ by $\pm1$, and $$[J^+,J^-]\ket{j,m}=\big(N_m^2-N_{m+1}^2\big)\ket{j,m}=m\ket{j,m}.$$ As $(2j{+}1)\times(2j{+}1)$ matrices, the rows and columns can be labelled directly by $m=j,j-1,\dots,-j$, or by position $p=1,\dots,2j+1$: $$\big[J_a^{(j)}\big]_{pq} = \bra{j,\,j+1-p}J_a\ket{j,\,j+1-q},$$ i.e. position $p$ corresponds to the $J_3$ eigenvalue $m=j+1-p$:

    $p$$1$$2$$3$$\cdots$$2j+1$
    $m$$j$$j-1$$j-2$$\cdots$$-j$
    Check — spin $\tfrac12$

    For $j=\tfrac12$ the only non-zero ladder element is $N_{1/2}=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\sqrt{1\cdot1}=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$, so $J^{+(1/2)}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1/\sqrt2\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ and $J^{-(1/2)}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\1/\sqrt2&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Then $$J_1^{(1/2)}=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad J_2^{(1/2)}=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad J_3^{(1/2)}=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},$$ i.e. $J_a^{(1/2)}=\tfrac12\sigma_a$, the Pauli matrices, as expected.
